Earthquakes GEOLOGICAL HAZARD Understand why the ground shakes and how this affects communities.

UNDERSTANDING THE HAZARD

What is an earthquake?

An earthquake is ground shaking caused by a sudden release of energy inside the Earth. That energy travels through rock as seismic waves. Earthquakes differ in size, depth and location, so the shaking experienced at the surface is not the same everywhere.

Most earthquakes result from movement along faults. The three explanations below describe different sources of earthquakes worldwide; they are not three stages that every earthquake follows.

Three causes to explore

Select a heading to read the explanation.

01Movement along tectonic faults

Tectonic plates move slowly, but friction can prevent the rocks on either side of a fault from sliding smoothly. Stress then builds in the surrounding rock.

When that stress overcomes resistance, the fault slips suddenly. Released energy spreads as seismic waves. This explains why slow plate movement can produce a brief, powerful episode of shaking.

02Movement of magma beneath volcanoes

Moving magma can change pressure beneath a volcano and fracture nearby rock. These fractures release energy and produce volcanic earthquakes.

This is a different setting from ordinary tectonic fault movement. Including it here does not mean that volcanic activity caused a particular earthquake in Myanmar.

03Human changes to underground pressure

Some earthquakes are induced by human activities. Injecting fluid deep underground can raise pressure on a connected fault, reducing the friction resisting movement.

A fault must already be in suitable conditions for this to happen. Most injection wells are not associated with felt earthquakes, so an industrial activity alone is not evidence of the cause.

Why the damage differs between places

Shaking depends on the earthquake and the ground it travels through. Building construction also matters: structures exposed to similar shaking can perform differently. Damage may affect homes, roads and buried services.

Ground failure can add to these effects. Shaking may trigger landslides or cause loose, water-saturated sediments to lose strength, a process called liquefaction. This is why damage cannot be explained by earthquake magnitude alone.

Landslides GEOLOGICAL HAZARD Explore why slopes become unstable and material moves downhill.

UNDERSTANDING THE HAZARD

What is a landslide?

A landslide is the downhill movement of rock, soil or debris under gravity. It may involve falling rocks, sliding ground or a flowing mixture of water and loose material. Some movements are slow; others develop rapidly.

A slope fails when the forces moving material downhill exceed the strength holding it in place. The following factors can trigger movement or make a slope less stable, and more than one can act together.

Three causes to explore

Select a heading to read the explanation.

01Heavy rainfall and saturated ground

Rainwater enters spaces within soil and fractured rock. As water pressure increases, the contact forces that help particles resist movement can decrease.

A slope already close to failure may then move. Rainfall duration, intensity and existing ground conditions all matter; the same amount of rain does not produce identical effects on every hillside.

02Earthquake shaking

Earthquake shaking places additional forces on a slope and can dislodge weakened rock or soil. Steep slopes and loose materials may be particularly susceptible.

The resulting landslide is a secondary hazard: the earthquake provides a trigger, while gravity carries the material downhill.

03Erosion and changes to a slope

A river can erode the bottom of a slope, removing support. Excavation, vegetation removal or altered drainage can also change slope stability.

For example, directing water onto a hillside can increase saturation. These changes may combine with rainfall or shaking, so a site investigation is needed to assess a particular slope.

How slope failure affects communities

Moving material can bury homes and block roads, railways or utilities. Even slow movement can gradually damage foundations and pipelines. The consequences depend on the movement's speed, size and the people or structures in its path.

A blocked route can interrupt services beyond the landslide itself. Some landslides also obstruct rivers, creating additional water-related hazards. Understanding the terrain is therefore as important as identifying the original trigger.

Subsidence & Sinkholes GEOLOGICAL HAZARD Understand why the ground can settle, sink or collapse.

UNDERSTANDING THE HAZARD

What are subsidence and sinkholes?

Subsidence means the ground surface moves downward. It can affect a broad area gradually. A sinkhole is a more local depression or opening associated with material being removed or support being lost beneath the surface.

The two are related but not identical: sinking ground does not always form an open hole. Different underground materials and processes produce different forms of movement.

Three causes to explore

Select a heading to read the explanation.

01Water dissolving underground rock

Groundwater can slowly dissolve soluble rocks such as limestone or gypsum. Spaces develop beneath the surface as this material is removed.

The overlying ground may settle into the space or remain intact until its support becomes insufficient. Collapse can then create a sinkhole, sometimes suddenly.

02Groundwater withdrawal and compaction

Removing groundwater can reduce water pressure within an aquifer. In susceptible sediments, the grains compress more tightly under the weight above them.

This compaction can lower the land surface across a wide area. It is a mechanism of subsidence and does not require a large underground cave or an open sinkhole.

03Underground excavation and lost support

Underground mining removes material that previously helped support the ground. If the remaining supports or the roof of an opening fail, the surface above can settle or collapse.

This differs from natural rock dissolution. The depth, shape and stability of underground workings influence whether movement reaches the surface.

Why ground movement matters

Uneven settlement can damage foundations, roads and buried pipes. A sudden collapse can create a local opening, while gradual subsidence may distort structures over a longer period.

The appearance of the surface alone does not identify the cause. Geological conditions, groundwater changes and underground activities must be considered together when investigating a particular location.

Tsunamis COASTAL HAZARD Explore how sudden water displacement creates powerful waves.

UNDERSTANDING THE HAZARD

What is a tsunami?

A tsunami is a series of long waves generated when a large volume of water is displaced suddenly. Unlike ordinary wind waves, a tsunami involves movement through a substantial part of the water column.

Waves spread from the source and may cross an ocean. At the coast, they can produce rapid flooding and strong currents. The causes below are examples rather than a complete list.

Three causes to explore

Select a heading to read the explanation.

01Sudden movement of the seabed

A large earthquake beneath or near the ocean can lift or lower part of the seabed. This moves the water above it and sends waves outward.

Not every underwater earthquake produces a significant tsunami. The location, size and movement of the rupture determine how much water is displaced.

02Landslides entering or moving beneath water

A large landslide can push water aside as it enters the sea or moves along the seabed. The volume and speed of the moving material affect the resulting waves.

These waves can be especially dangerous near their source and may reach nearby shores quickly. Landslide-generated tsunamis generally lose energy faster than large earthquake-generated tsunamis.

03Volcanic eruptions and collapse

Some volcanic activity near water can cause sudden displacement. Examples include a volcano's flank collapsing into the sea, an underwater explosion or volcanic material rapidly entering water.

The eruption itself is not enough to explain the waves: a process must transfer energy to the water. These mechanisms are uncommon compared with earthquake sources.

How coastal impacts develop

Fast-moving water, debris and erosion can damage buildings, boats and coastal infrastructure. Flooding may extend inland, and strong currents can remain dangerous after the first wave has arrived.

Coastal shape, seabed depth and land elevation affect local impacts. A tsunami is a series of waves, so a single photograph of one wave cannot describe the full event or its duration.

Flooding HYDROLOGICAL HAZARD Understand how excess water spreads onto normally dry land.

UNDERSTANDING THE HAZARD

What is flooding?

Flooding occurs when water covers normally dry land. It can develop through intense rainfall, rising rivers or seawater moving inland. Several processes may overlap during one event.

Three causes to explore

Select a heading to read the explanation.

01Rainfall exceeding absorption and drainage

Intense rain can accumulate faster than soil absorbs it or drains remove it. Water then collects or flows across the surface.

Paved areas reduce infiltration, while already saturated soil has less capacity to absorb additional rainfall.

02Rivers overflowing their banks

Rain across a river's catchment feeds water into its channels. When river levels exceed the banks, water spreads across the floodplain.

Flooding can therefore occur downstream from the heaviest rainfall, even where local rain is limited.

03Storm-driven coastal water

Storm winds can push seawater towards land, raising coastal water levels and flooding low-lying areas.

This storm surge is different from a tsunami. Rainfall and river flooding may occur during the same storm.

How communities are affected

Floodwater can affect homes, farmland and transport routes. The depth, speed, duration and location of flooding influence the damage.

Interrupted roads can isolate communities, making an event's consequences extend beyond the land directly covered by water.
